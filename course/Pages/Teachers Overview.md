This page provides an explanation of the Complex Software Systems semester (30 EC). Its purpose is to give a quick insight into the structure of the semester, the educational vision, the division of roles, and the key moments at which alignment, supervision, and assessment take place.

## Purpose and Educational Vision

The *Complex Software Systems* semester is designed to have students work on authentic, complex software challenges in collaboration with external stakeholders (Partners in Innovation / research groups). 

Core principles:  

- Engineering mindset: working in progressive steps with short feedback cycles  
- Systems thinking: attention to the coherence between architecture, data, processes, and stakeholders  
- Quality & maintainability as a structural part of design and implementation  
- Room for individual learning paths through individual experiments (personal flavours)  

For lecturers, this means: 

- Focus on process, rationale, and decision-making.  
- Being a role model in demonstrating core principles in your coaching and if applicable, let students look over the shoulder with your own experimental and research activities.
- Stimulating collaboration and systems thinking 
- Using feedback as the primary steering instrument 
- Encouraging students to engage in self-reflection and professional orientation 

## Lecturers, Roles, and Responsibilities

Within the semester, three lecturer roles are distinguished. Depending on staffing, roles may be combined.

### Team Coach

Focus: project execution, professional attitude, and stakeholder interaction 

Responsibilities: 

- Facilitating group processes and Agile ways of working 
- Aligning with external stakeholders 
- Stimulating community building 
- Providing input for holistic assessment

### Technical Coach

Focus: software engineering, architecture, quality, and experimentation 

Responsibilities: 

- Guiding technical choices and experiments 
- Encouraging research-oriented and experimental work 
- Facilitating technical workshops 
- Reflecting on the impact of technical decisions

### Semester Coach

Focus: personal development and professional identity 

Responsibilities: 

- Holding individual conversations about growth, reflection, and future direction 
- Supervising Personal Leadership learning outcomes 
- Aligning with other coaches for assessment purposes

## Semester Structure

The semester consists of two parallel learning activities: 

- Group Project (GP) – the main time investment 
- Individual Experiments (IE / Personal Flavours) – deepening or broadening 

Within this context, students develop the knowledge, skills, and professional attitude as described in the learning outcomes and apply them in practice. Workshops, community activities, and fixed feedback moments support this development. 

The semester is divided into sprints of approximately three weeks. The exact planning is aligned by each project group with the client and coaches.

## Educational Components

### Group Project

Students work in teams of 4–6 on an authentic assignment for an external stakeholder. Key characteristics include: 

1. An Agile way of working 
2. Focus on both functional and non-functional requirements 
3. A combination of implementation, research, and advisory work 

Role of lecturers: 

- Assisting in formulating research questions 
- Stimulating collaboration (within the group, with the client, and with other project teams) 
- Challenging architectural and quality-related decisions 
- Validating intermediate products for the portfolio 
- Safeguarding professional standards   

### Individual Experiment / Personal Flavour

Individual experiments provide students with the opportunity to deepen their expertise in a self-chosen domain (e.g. cloud-native, data-driven, cybersecurity, agentic AI). 

Objectives: 

- Deepening core software engineering skills 
- Making individual competencies visible 
- Experimenting through rapid prototyping 
- Personalizing the educational program 

Role of lecturers: 

- Supervising the research and experimental setup 
- Focusing on rationale and learning outcomes 
- Providing feedback on process and reflection (not only on the final result)

## Assessment

Assessment is holistic and longitudinal. Key principles include: 

- Everything counts: products, process, professionalism, and development 
- Feedback is leading; assessment follows 
- Students actively collect feedback themselves (feedback triangulation) 
- Development-oriented feedback scale: undefined, orienting, beginning, proficient, advanced 
- Minimum rule: if all learning outcomes are demonstrated at least at proficient level, the result is satisfactory or higher. All other assessments are discussed in the assessor meeting, where the final, joint assessment takes place

> **Additional explanation: Students actively collect feedback (feedback triangulation)**
> In previous semesters, students were accustomed to requesting feedback primarily from their assigned coach(es). In this semester, students are expected to actively seek out relevant, additional feedback. 
> Each student follows a different learning path and therefore needs to consciously consider which expertise is of added value. This skill is taken into account in both supervision and assessment.

## Study plan  

Study Plan Each student starts the semester by creating a study plan. This plan is part of the portfolio and describes how the student purposefully works toward achieving the learning outcomes, aligned with personal ambitions, starting position, and project context. 

Purpose of the study plan The study plan helps students translate learning outcomes into concrete learning goals and actions, make conscious choices regarding focus, depth, and experimentation, and take ownership of their learning process. It also supports students in collecting and effectively using targeted feedback. 

The study plan is not a static document, but a living development plan that is discussed and refined throughout the semester based on feedback and progressive insight. 

Contents of the study plan A study plan contains at least: 

- Starting position: self-assessment per learning outcome (where am I now?) 
- Learning goals: personal emphases within the semester learning outcomes 
- Approach and activities: contribution to the group project, the setup of the individual experiments and personal flavours, and learning activities (workshops, self-study, community) 
- Evidence and products: what do I show in my portfolio, and why? 
- Feedback strategy: from whom do I request feedback, and when? 
- Reflection points: how do I evaluate my progress? 
- Relation to the group project and individual experiments 

Role of lecturers Lecturers use the study plan as a conversation tool during coaching sessions and as a reference when providing targeted feedback or assessing choices and evidence in the portfolio. They question students on their rationale and feasibility, help them maintain focus (not everything at once), and view the study plan as guiding rather than contractual. 

Development throughout the semester The study plan is created at the start of the semester, discussed and refined during the first portfolio review, and adjusted after feedback moments when necessary.

## Portfolio & Tools

Students use their portfolio to:

- document and demonstrate their development; 
- collect validated professional products; 
- share reflections linked to learning outcomes; 
- collect documented feedback from lecturers, peers, and experts. 

Tools: 

- Portflow
- Feedpulse

## Community Building

Community is an explicit learning objective of the semester. Examples of community activities include: 

- Software Design Meetings 
- Personal Experiment Meetings 
- Poster presentations 
- Innovation Insights 

Lecturers are responsible for stimulating community building and aligning interaction toward knowledge sharing. They do so by: connecting students and group projects in various accessible ways; explicitly articulating the value of these interactions and leading by example—being visible and approachable in the learning environment and coordinating with others. 

It is also possible to outsource part of the project. This requires alignment between project supervisors and the project groups involved.

## Practical Arrangements 

- Communication primarily takes place via the LMS environment 
- Lecturer teams regularly align through intervision and calibration 
- Milestones are coordinated and communicated well in advance with students (e.g. by documenting them in Canvas)
- Shared responsibility for consistency and quality of supervision
